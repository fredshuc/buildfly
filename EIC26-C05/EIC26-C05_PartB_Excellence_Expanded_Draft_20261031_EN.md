AirTransformer Resilience develops climate-resilient cooling and atmospheric water harvesting for heat-exposed urban facilities. Staged sorbents use compatible low-grade heat for regeneration, with heat rejection matched to the site. The product will deliver an assigned air-side cooling duty and water qualified for actual non-potable demand. # 1 Technology

## 1.1 Novelty and breakthrough nature

Savery is developing AirTransformer to deliver reliable cooling and atmospheric water harvesting for facilities operating under high temperatures, constrained electrical capacity and limited water supplies. Customers receive cooling at a specified air temperature and duty, with humidity requirements defined at the delivery interface, together with recovered water for qualified non-potable uses such as cooling or equipment make-up. Low-grade heat restores the water-capturing sorbents. The proposed breakthrough is to design complementary metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), their contactors and their operating trajectories together, then adapt those trajectories as conditions change. This creates a route to simultaneous cooling and useful water recovery over a wider operating envelope, with manufacturing specifications derived from the physical behaviour needed to deliver that service.

The scientific challenge is to make a material's reversible uptake accessible at the rate and operating conditions required by the installation. Selecting the largest equilibrium capacity does not determine how much cooling a contactor can sustain before it must be restored. Equally, a controller cannot recover a state that the material, heat source or heat sink makes physically inaccessible. Our design therefore treats material choice, the shaped contactor and the sequence of reachable states as coupled variables. The intended advance is a reproducible method for choosing that combination and adapting its operation while preserving the specified service. Its success will be judged on complete repeated cycles and the operating envelope of manufactured equipment. [R01–R03; I01]

### Cooling and water recovery within one system

The proposed configuration couples thermally managed, staged MOF air treatment to indirect evaporative cooling. MOFs capture water vapour; heat exchange removes adsorption heat so that the resulting air state supports the cooling process. Evaporation in a working-air path then extracts sensible heat through an exchanger from the delivered-air path. The two streams remain separated, and humid working air is discharged separately, preventing evaporated water from being added to the delivered air. Useful cooling is measured at that delivery interface. Adsorption heat management, moisture capture and evaporative heat removal therefore perform distinct, connected functions.

Contactors alternate between adsorption and regeneration. An independent regeneration path supplies heat to release captured water and restore working capacity. A condenser-and-collection branch will recover water from the regeneration-side humid flow or vapour stream. Condensation requires compatible water-vapour partial pressure and heat-sink temperature; the resulting liquid passes to treatment and storage for its intended use. Sensible heat can be recovered between cooling and warming components, while other internal thermal transfers are useful where temperature differences and cycle timing support them. Unrecovered adsorption and condensation heat must reach a defined sink. All electrical auxiliaries required to deliver the service are included, covering cooling, regeneration, heat rejection, controls and water treatment.

Water accounting distinguishes atmospheric water captured, qualified liquid recovered, internal consumption and net water available to other site uses. Recovered water used within the cooling process can displace external make-up water even when no surplus is exported. Evaporation in cooling and heat-rejection equipment, cleaning and treatment losses enter the same balance. Site-specific water qualification and comparison at equal cooling service establish the actual supply substitution. Operation prioritises required cooling within the qualified envelope, adapting water recovery to humidity, available regeneration heat and heat rejection.

The development configuration makes continuity explicit. Two independently assembled banks, each containing two serial material stages, will alternate between air treatment and restoration. While one bank treats air, the other must regenerate and recover sufficiently to take over. They share the cooling, condensation, treatment and measurement interfaces of one integration rig. Regeneration and recovery must fit within the opposite bank's available treatment time. Switching gaps, incomplete restoration and any incumbent-cooling contribution remain in the service record; no additional buffer is assumed. A material that captures more water can therefore reduce useful service if restoring it delays the next bank. The proposed joint design addresses that interaction directly. [I01, T2.2–T2.4]

Heat, humidity and heat rejection impose different limits. A hot-dry period can increase the importance of cooling continuity while reducing the water available for capture. A wetter inlet increases the moisture load, but useful liquid recovery still depends on regeneration and condenser conditions. A warmer sink can restrict both heat rejection and condensation. We will map the configurations and modes that remain feasible under these combined conditions. The intended resilience benefit is the service retained within the site's actual utility constraints, with heat, electricity and net water use separately accounted for. Peak cooling and peak water recovery will not be assumed to coincide. [I01, T1.1–T1.2; T2.4]

AirJoule publicly describes sorbent contactors, regeneration, condensation and dry-air output [R04a]. Savery's proposed technical contribution connects material pairing and accessible operating conditions to measured dissipation, adaptive cycle decisions and reproducible manufacture. The development addresses how cooling and water service remain feasible together as climate, demand and manufactured properties vary.

### Complementary MOFs create additional operating paths

A MOF's uptake varies with temperature and water-vapour chemical potential. Its adsorption step identifies the window in which a small change in conditions produces a large loading change. Chemistry can tune that window, as demonstrated for mixed-linker aluminium MOFs [R01]. For a working machine, the decisive quantity is cyclic working capacity between feasible capture and release states. The heat source, heat sink, inlet humidity and hysteresis determine that capacity. A material with high equilibrium uptake can retain too much water after regeneration or respond too slowly to provide useful power.

In a staged train, the first contactor can remove the bulk moisture load from a wetter inlet. This changes the chemical potential encountered downstream, where a complementary material works in the drier window created upstream. Stronger water affinity can extend drying but also changes the temperature and vapour conditions required for release and subsequent condensation. Each material completes its own capture, release and restoration cycle. Assigning the load between stages therefore requires joint selection of adsorption windows, cyclic capacities, regeneration conditions and thermal interfaces. The downstream material must operate on the air state that the upstream stage can actually supply throughout the cycle.

Material pairing, stage order, shaped geometry and recovery timing together determine the paths available to the machine. Changing an upstream window can reduce a downstream load; changing thermal coupling can make a regeneration transition feasible within the available time and heat supply. Conversely, an unsuitable pairing can strand capacity or require excessive heating. We will select combinations using these coupled effects, comparing them with a single-material design at matched service, boundary conditions and a declared resource budget, such as installed contactor volume. Sorbent inventory, exchanger area and pressure drop will be reported explicitly. This makes the system contribution of complementary materials measurable.

The material hypothesis concerns the evolving air state along the train. The upstream stage must supply a humidity trajectory within the second material's useful capture window, while both stages retain reachable release and recovery states. We will therefore assess the second stage against the outlet history of the first, rather than rank both materials at one common inlet condition. A pairing that improves drying but imposes incompatible regeneration conditions, excessive pressure loss or delayed bank availability will be rejected or assigned a narrower operating domain. Complementarity is useful only when it survives this whole-cycle test.

Material and control contributions will be distinguished experimentally. Paired and same-material arrangements will first operate under a common rules strategy with equal tuning allowance and declared service and resource conditions. Rules, model-predictive control (MPC) and reinforcement learning (RL) will then be compared on the selected complete configuration. This sequence tests whether an advantage comes from the material paths made available, from how those paths are traversed, or from their interaction. An advantage confined to particular source, humidity or sink conditions will be reported with those conditions. [I01, T2.3; T3.4]

### Measured dissipation guides finite-time design

The dynamic model tracks contactor temperature, sorbent loading, local vapour pressure, air humidity and flow, and the thermal inventory of connected exchangers. Conduction, thermal contact resistance, intraparticle diffusion, gas-side transfer and pressure drop determine how fast these states can move. As uptake approaches equilibrium, declining driving force can make the final increment disproportionately slow. Shortening adsorption can raise cycle frequency but leave capacity unused; prolonging heat recovery can reduce subsequent heating demand but delay the next available contactor. Useful power depends on the complete sequence, including switching and restoration.

Prior research co-authored by Frédéric S. Cui provides an experimental basis for evaluating this trade-off. Henninger et al. (2023) [R02] used large-temperature-jump experiments on CAU-10-H and aluminium fumarate, relating progress through adsorption to characteristic times and mass-, area- and volume-specific mean powers. The results show why comparing only a few time constants can give misleading material rankings. AirTransformer development extends that reasoning from small-scale sorption measurements to coupled stages, heat recovery and complete cooling and water-service trajectories. The published work establishes a method for evaluating finite-time behaviour; the integrated service is the development advance pursued here.

Our method will construct a local dissipation representation from measured transport and relaxation behaviour. Temperature and vapour-pressure step responses identify rate limitations; heat and mass balances constrain the fitted dynamics. At each relevant state and action, thermal gradients, diffusion, pressure losses, hysteresis and switching transients contribute to the cost of moving to the next state. The representation identifies where additional speed is expensive and where useful transfer can proceed with smaller losses. Near linear response, a friction metric provides the thermodynamic-length description; calibrated nonlinear dynamics and explicit transition losses cover larger excursions and switching [R03].

Identification will distinguish physical rate limitations before the design search assigns a benefit to changing them. Temperature and vapour steps, flow changes, and paths with different durations and switching orders will be combined with thickness and thermal-contact variants. Parameter estimates will carry covariance, validity ranges and conditioning or ageing state. Sensitivity and observability checks will establish which quantities the measurements can resolve. If different prior histories produce different responses to the same perturbation, the states or sensing must be revised before the model is used to select a trajectory. This tests whether the model captures the memory that matters for subsequent operation. [I01, T1.3; T2.2; T3.1]

The thermodynamic-length construction is a local tool within that measured description. Small separate and combined perturbations will test the linear-response region and identify a positive-semidefinite friction representation where supported. Coupled temperature and vapour changes may require cross terms; estimating separate response times alone will not establish the complete metric. The linear-response basis of [R03] does not establish a global optimum for every industrial trajectory. Larger excursions, hysteresis and switching will retain calibrated nonlinear dynamics and explicit transition losses, with the validity of each representation tested against reserved paths. [I01, T3.2]

This measured representation enters the design search itself. Candidate material pairs define accessible sorption windows, while shaping, exchanger contact and stage connections change the rates and losses between them. The search jointly selects these properties and the finite-time sequence of adsorption, recovery and regeneration. Its objective is to minimise total entropy generation within the defined system boundary at specified source and sink conditions, subject to prescribed cooling service, the operating mode's requirement for qualified water recovery or external-water substitution, cycle duration, equipment limits, and available heat and electricity. Comparable initial and final loading and thermal inventories prevent apparent improvement through depletion of stored capacity. Thermal input, electrical demand and service outputs remain separate engineering measures.

The loss reconstruction will use a control volume covering both banks, indirect evaporative cooling, heat recovery, condensation, treatment and auxiliaries. It will reconcile entropy storage, air and water crossing the boundary, and heat exchanged at the corresponding boundary temperatures. Electrical work enters the energy balance without an entropy flow. Internal heat transfers are not external entropy flows; the entropy generated by them remains included in the whole-system reconstruction. Local loss allocations will therefore not be added a second time. Contributions that cannot be separately identified will remain grouped or bounded. This keeps the design objective tied to a measurable installation boundary and makes the uncertainty of the comparison explicit. [I01, T3.2]

Lower entropy generation is meaningful here only alongside the required service and matched terminal inventories. Ending a test with a wetter sorbent or a depleted thermal store can shift restoration demand beyond the observation period. Within the common evaluation period, comparisons will retain actual cycle timing and require the specified terminal inventories. A terminal-state shortfall will remain visible rather than be hidden by unreported post-test restoration. Heat and electrical consumption will remain separate, since the available heat temperature and the site's electrical limit impose different constraints. Useful cooling and qualified water output will be reported with those inputs, rather than compressed into an unexplained combined efficiency. [I01, T1.2; T3.4]

The resulting trajectories allocate time where it sustains useful transfer. They can end a low-output adsorption tail earlier, coordinate restoration of the next contactor, or recover sensible heat before it is rejected. Benefits will be assessed through delivered cooling power, qualified water recovery, heat and electricity use, and disturbance recovery time. Complete repeated-cycle comparisons will include switching losses, balance closure and uncertainty. The same feasible trajectories and local loss representation then guide online control, linking laboratory measurements to decisions made under changing site conditions.

### Reconfiguration and learning across successive cycles

Reconfiguration provides choices over active contactors, flow allocation, adsorption and regeneration durations, regeneration temperature and supported heat-recovery connections. Flow and phase timing principally affect transfer rates and the time available for them. Temperature setpoints and thermal allocation alter the driving conditions relative to each material's sorption window. These effects remain physically coupled: increasing flow changes both transfer coefficients and pressure losses, while changing heat allocation affects temperature and regeneration rate. Distinguishing their contributions enables the controller to choose how to accelerate a process without imposing an unnecessarily costly driving condition.

Temperature, humidity, pressure, flow and condensate measurements will feed a model-based estimator of loading, thermal inventory and slowly changing transfer parameters. A faster surrogate will retain mass and energy conservation and admissible states, with estimation uncertainty carried into operating decisions. A contactor that appears available by elapsed time may still hold excessive water or heat. Its estimated state determines whether to extend regeneration, allocate cooling flow elsewhere or change the next switching time. Parameter estimates also connect accepted manufacturing variation to the trajectories available on each unit.

Physics-constrained reinforcement learning will form the adaptive policy for these decisions over successive cycles. Additional adsorption now increases captured water but can lengthen subsequent regeneration; longer recovery can save future heat while postponing cooling capacity. Hysteresis, thermal inventory and changing demand make the consequences depend on operating history. The policy will use the measured loss representation and feasible operating domain to minimise dissipation over successive cycles while meeting the cooling duty, the mode-specific water requirement and the site's resource limits. Training in calibrated models will span climate, load and accepted manufacturing variation, followed by controlled experimental evaluation. This gives learning a specific role in coordinating delayed consequences across changing sequences of feasible states.

Temperature, pressure, actuator and delivered-service limits will be enforced as hard constraints outside the reward. Rule-based and model-predictive control will provide matched comparators and qualified fallback operation when state confidence or operating-domain limits are exceeded. All policies will use the same available sensors, actuators, disturbances and service requirements, allowing the contribution of learned adaptation to be assessed against predictive as well as fixed control.

The policy's objective will accumulate estimated entropy generation over each action's actual duration and over a common evaluation period. An adsorption extension that looks favourable immediately must also pay for its later effect on regeneration and bank availability. Service, heat, electricity and terminal-inventory requirements remain explicit constraints. This makes the control problem depend on the consequences of decisions across cycles and avoids rewarding a policy simply for taking shorter steps or reporting a favourable fragment of operation. The role of learning is to improve that sequential allocation within the identified domain. [I01, T3.3]

Complete trajectories will be reserved for final evaluation separately from model fitting and policy training. A frozen, measurement-constrained loss reconstruction and uncertainty method will evaluate those records independently of the training surrogate. Rules and MPC will receive the same hardware, sensors, actuator rights, demand histories and evaluation periods as RL. Service deficits, constraint activation, fallback and adverse outcomes remain visible. A dissipation advantage that cannot be resolved within the reconstruction uncertainty will remain unresolved; actual cooling, water and resource measurements will still define the supported engineering conclusion. Qualified fallback provides protection, while the additional value of learning requires its own comparative result. [I01, T3.3–T3.4]

### Manufacturing specifications preserve the operating capability

Coating thickness, binder fraction, porosity, shaped geometry, thermal bonding and sealing determine accessible capacity, transfer rates, pressure drop and leakage. Two contactors with the same MOF mass can therefore support different operating trajectories. Manufacturing qualification will measure these effective properties and propagate their variation through the dynamic model. Required service trajectories will, in turn, define acceptable process windows and parameter ranges. Material chemistry and stage selection remain part of this loop alongside shaping and assembly.

A proposed MOF synthesis-shaping co-optimisation platform will connect controlled synthesis and post-treatment with shaping and interface preparation for the existing C05 material candidates. Its functional modules support traceable process windows, batch-to-batch consistency, and measurements of shaped-material adsorption, transport, structure and contact behaviour. T2.1 retains three chemistries, two shaping routes and two independent preparations; T5.1 connects the resulting process records to contactor instructions and tolerances. T1.3 establishes measurement traceability, while T2.2 and T3.2 test how processing changes relaxation, feasible trajectories and contactor suitability. Equipment quantities, capacities, suppliers and prices remain unverified. This capability is proposed for C05 reproducibility and integration; it establishes neither present performance nor starting TRL. [C01]

The empirical reason to connect chemistry and processing is already visible in the published coating work. Henninger et al. report differences between coating and granular equilibrium behaviour and show that rankings depend on the loading interval and mass, area or volume basis. Their tests used pure water vapour without carrier gas. The results motivate testing the processed contactor and its chosen cycle, while humid-air transport and delivered cooling require C05-specific verification. [R02, §§3.2/4.1/4.3]

The platform will link controlled synthesis, separation and washing, activation and conditioning, shaping and interface preparation, post-shaping verification and traceable records. These functional groups form a proposed process sequence, not six selected instruments. Each candidate will be evaluated after the processing that determines its intended use. Its preparation record will be connected to the shaped material's accessible adsorption window, relaxation, structure and contact behaviour, and then to the bank's feasible trajectory. This provides the data needed to investigate why a promising powder succeeds or fails after it becomes a working contactor. [C01; I01, T2.1–T2.2]

Co-optimisation makes the feedback reciprocal. A dynamic design may identify a promising chemistry whose shaped contactor cannot restore its capacity in the available time. Process development can then test whether another feasible shaping or interface route changes the limiting transport behaviour while preserving cyclic capacity and mechanical suitability. Conversely, favourable uptake can be outweighed by poor repeatability, pressure loss or thermal contact. Selection will therefore return a compatible material, process, geometry and operating domain. The sensitivity of service to those properties will inform manufacturing tolerances and the measurements required for factory release. [I01, T3.2; T5.1]

The screen remains three chemistries, two feasible shaping routes and two independent preparations: twelve initial specimens. This supports candidate selection and the first process-response comparison. Later qualification lots will examine initial consistency and defects; neither stage establishes the full production distribution or long-term durability. Factory calibration will remain within the validated parameter domain. A contactor outside the qualified process or functional limits will require investigation and, where necessary, requalification; controller adaptation cannot by itself establish its manufacturing conformity. [I01, T2.1; T5.1–T5.2]

Within those qualified windows, bounded calibration will match control parameters to each unit. Factory tests will measure cyclic capacity and dynamic response, verify pressure drop and leakage, and check the integrated cooling and water interfaces under specified conditions. Traceability will connect material batches, process records, identified model parameters, software versions and release results. EU-based qualification of critical processes, integrated assembly and testing will retain this feedback where manufacturing decisions are made. Repeated production can then improve process control using evidence from both factory and operating measurements.

AirTransformer's advance is the ability to select and reproduce a useful thermodynamic path for the conditions a customer actually faces. Complementary materials define the available windows; measured dynamics determine how to traverse them within service time; adaptive control allocates capacity as conditions change. Manufacturing and release testing will establish which of those capabilities each delivered machine can sustain. Together, these mechanisms support repeatable cooling and atmospheric water recovery across a defined range of low-grade heat sources, heat sinks and climate conditions.

## 2.1 Market opportunity

### Customers and purchase decisions

AirTransformer addresses facilities that must maintain cooling through high temperatures while managing constrained electrical capacity and water supplies. Customers buy complete equipment and associated system service for a specified air-side cooling duty, together with qualified recovered water or reduced external water demand. Savery initially targets data centres and campuses where usable heat, cooling demand and an operational use for water coincide.

In our target customer profile, the owner or developer controls investment; facilities engineers specify interfaces; procurement evaluates delivery and contractual responsibility; and the operator accepts and maintains the installation. Savery's application engineering will translate the assigned duty into agreed thermal, electrical and water boundaries, commissioning tests and operating responsibilities. Two buying situations guide entry. Existing data centres and campuses can retrofit or expand a defined air-side duty using available heat, seeking lower electrical demand and external make-up water consumption with manageable installation disruption. Planned campuses can specify heat recovery, cooling and on-site water uses together, reducing interface compromises before construction. Both use the same equipment platform. AirTransformer's assigned duty remains distinct from GPU cold plates and the primary liquid-cooling loop; adjacent applications include campus ventilation and selected process-air duties.

Qualification requires compatible source temperature, capacity and timing, an adequate heat sink, suitable air and humidity conditions, and an identified cooling load. Space, pressure drop, retrofit connections, operating hours and maintenance access also matter. Recovered water must meet its intended use; treatment, evaporation, cleaning and heat-rejection consumption determine net substitution or export. Existing condensate recovery and reuse belong in the comparison. Humid water-recovery conditions and dry heatwaves need not coincide, so qualification covers the operating modes that matter across the year.

The proposed value comes from matching complementary material windows to changing air states and adapting regeneration, recovery and switching across cycles. Staging distributes moisture loads across different adsorption windows, while state-based cycle timing limits low-output transfer and restores capacity for subsequent cooling. This aims to sustain useful cooling and water service when heat, humidity and load vary, extending the conditions in which available heat can economically replace electrical cooling demand. Customer net operating value is baseline annual cost minus Savery's cost at equal service, including electricity, heat, heat rejection, water supply and treatment, and maintenance. That value must justify installation and capital costs. Avoided utility expansion is counted separately only against an identified alternative investment; it is not added again to market expenditure.

## 2.3 Broader impact

AirTransformer is intended to preserve a facility's assigned air-side cooling service when high temperatures coincide with electricity and water constraints. Where humidity, usable heat and heat rejection permit, thermally regenerated sorbents can take on moisture treatment that would otherwise consume electrical cooling capacity. Complementary stages distribute that load across suitable adsorption windows; coordinated recovery and switching restore contactors for subsequent duty. The intended result is more cooling available within the site's electrical limit and fewer interruptions as conditions change. Operators gain a way to sustain the service on which their facilities depend, while occupants and infrastructure users benefit through continued operation of the cooled spaces and systems.

Qualified recovered water adds a second resource benefit. The initial F1 field use is segregated, treated, non-aerosol equipment cleaning, subject to use qualification and the site's actual demand [I01]. Qualified production, internal consumption, water actually used for that duty, net export, storage changes and discarded water are recorded separately. Cooling remains the priority within the qualified operating envelope, with recovery adjusted to humidity, regeneration and condenser conditions. Any subsequent cooling make-up use requires its own assessment. The external-resource benefit is the reduction in total withdrawal relative to the reference installation, including treatment, cleaning, evaporation and heat-rejection consumption; its significance depends on local and seasonal water conditions.

Two comparisons distinguish continuity from efficiency. Resilience uses the same cooling demand, weather and external-interface conditions, with capacities and available utility limits stated for each installation. Actual delivery may differ: compliant hours, unmet cooling and recovery reveal the service retained under constraints. Resource and lifecycle comparisons instead require equivalent delivered cooling, outlet conditions, evaluation periods and specified water service under the stated weather and interface conditions. If the reference cannot meet demand within its limits, its service shortfall remains visible; supplementary cooling, external water or heat rejection needed for equal service is accounted for separately. Product-side regeneration, auxiliaries, treatment, heat rejection and retained plant enter the same complete-installation boundary.
