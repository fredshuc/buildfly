## Executive summary

AirTransformer provides cooling for facilities that must keep operating through urban heatwaves, electricity constraints and water stress. Savery Tech already has a complete TRL6 machine combining sorption air treatment, thermal management and indirect evaporative cooling. Its CAU-series material route uses low-temperature regeneration heat, with a product qualification envelope of 50–70°C heat supply, 16–20°C delivered air and a humidity ratio of 9–11 g/kg dry air under the applicable rating conditions. The productisation targets are 60–70% lower electricity demand and 40–60% lower external make-up water at equivalent cooling service; installation economics are screened against a 24–36-month payback target.

Savery owns its 2019 system patent family and controls the product architecture and engineering know-how. Its scientific board includes Norbert Stock and Aline Auroux. An awarded CETP grant supports the ongoing 2026–2029 project towards TRL7, with HPE providing the site. The EIC action builds on this basis to qualify repeatable manufacture, reliability, compliance and deployment at TRL8. The 24-month programme requests €2,485,583.33 grant against €3,550,833.33 eligible costs, plus €2m EIC investment within an €8m Seed round. The investment supports co-financing and commercial deployment, while the grant buys the distinct product evidence required for a repeatable European cooling business.

# 1 Technology

## 1.1 Novelty and breakthrough nature

### A working cooling product with a defined engineering advance

AirTransformer is a modular cooling and air-treatment product for facilities with a continuous heat load and access to suitable low-grade heat. Its first commercial focus is data-centre infrastructure, where high ambient temperatures can coincide with electricity-network constraints and restrictions on cooling-water supply. The product combines moisture removal by metal-organic framework sorbents with thermal management and indirect evaporative sensible cooling. It supplies conditioned air at the agreed delivery interface while the facility retains its existing cooling installation for redundancy and duties outside the module's operating envelope.

Savery has already integrated the core functions in a complete TRL6 machine. The present innovation effort concerns making this product dependable, manufacturable and economical across qualified installations. It addresses the engineering relationship between the CAU-series sorbent, its shaped and coated heat exchanger, the cyclic thermal process and the customer's required service. A material with attractive equilibrium uptake is useful only if the manufactured contactor can capture and release moisture quickly enough, with acceptable pressure loss, heat transfer and ageing. Similarly, an efficient contactor does not establish an efficient installation if regeneration, pumping or heat rejection consumes the benefit.

The breakthrough proposition is to deliver the required air service using a thermal resource that many facilities already reject, while reducing demand for electricity and external make-up water. Its defensible value lies in the complete installation: air-side performance, utility interfaces, repeatable production, maintenance and acceptance evidence. The action therefore advances an existing integrated machine through product engineering and qualification rather than beginning with an open search for a new sorbent chemistry. [T01, T02]

### How the system delivers cooling

The process-air path first passes through the sorption section. The CAU-series material captures water vapour and the thermal circuit removes the heat released by adsorption. Drying lowers the air's wet-bulb temperature and creates a more favourable driving condition for the downstream indirect evaporative section. In that section, water evaporates into a separate working-air stream and removes sensible heat across the exchanger. The cooled product-air stream remains separate from the humid exhaust, so evaporated water is not added to the delivered air.

The contactors alternate between treatment and regeneration. Low-grade heat restores the sorbent's working capacity, while switching and heat recovery coordinate the availability of the next contactor. Regeneration air, product air and working air have separate interfaces. The thermal design must accommodate both adsorption heat and the regeneration requirement; rejected heat must reach a compatible sink. Fans, pumps, actuators, controls and water treatment all contribute to the installation's electrical demand. These loads are included when comparing alternatives, together with any residual duty supplied by the retained plant.

The product's water benefit starts with reduced external make-up for the same delivered cooling. Atmospheric moisture that is recovered as liquid can contribute to that balance when the regeneration and condensation conditions allow it. The water ledger distinguishes capture, condensation, treatment losses, storage changes, discharge, internal consumption and net usable output. Recirculating an existing water inventory is not counted repeatedly as new production. Recovered liquid is qualified for its actual use, with isolated non-aerosol equipment cleaning as a possible initial application. The action makes no fixed daily water-output promise independent of humidity, heat-source availability and heat-sink conditions.

This arrangement gives the facility an additional operating choice. During heatwaves, the operator can allocate a defined air-treatment duty to AirTransformer while preserving incumbent cooling for peak or exceptional conditions. When available heat, humidity or sink temperature moves outside the qualified domain, the controller changes mode or bypasses the module. Cooling continuity therefore depends on a specified allocation and fallback arrangement, supported by a measurable service contract and commissioning test.

### CAU material processing and contactor manufacture

The current engineering material route uses CAU-series variants. Qualification will establish a controlled synthesis and conditioning window, preserve the adsorption response after shaping, and transfer the process to repeatable contactor manufacture. The relevant outputs are the material and process specification, an accepted batch, a reproducible coating or shaped structure and a tested replacement procedure. Broad chemical screening is not the starting point of the action.

Processing determines how much of the material's capacity remains accessible in the machine. Binder content, coating thickness, porosity, adhesion and thermal contact influence moisture-transfer rate, pressure loss and cycle duration. The process-development work will therefore measure performance after shaping and assembly, rather than release powder solely on an equilibrium isotherm. Incoming-material identity, preparation conditions, activation, substrate preparation and final conditioning will be traceable. Manufacturing acceptance will connect these records to adsorption response, airflow resistance, coating integrity and thermal performance.

The contactor also has to survive its intended operating environment. Condensation, particulate exposure, contamination and cleaning can change the surface and transfer behaviour. Qualification specimens and assembled units will undergo representative exposure and recovery sequences, followed by the same acceptance measurements used at release. Where performance changes materially, the response will be a revised operating or maintenance limit, process adjustment or replacement criterion. This creates a product quality system that can distinguish a manufacturing deviation from normal ageing and can support service decisions in the field.

Savery will use existing facilities where they meet the task. Additional equipment, specialist testing and external processing will be selected by their contribution to process control, product evidence and delivery capacity. The industrialisation output is a transferable manufacturing package comprising drawings, bills of material, tolerances, process instructions, inspection points, test methods and release criteria. Critical process knowledge remains under Savery's control, while qualified suppliers receive the information and permissions necessary for their assigned work.

### Finite-time design and measured transport

Savery's design capability connects sorption equilibrium to the time available in a working machine. The useful quantity is the moisture exchanged during a complete repeatable cycle, at the actual source and sink conditions, together with the cooling delivered during that time. A long adsorption tail may add little service while delaying regeneration; an early switch can increase cycle frequency but leave capacity unused. Heat recovery also trades reduced heating demand against the time needed to prepare the next contactor.

Published work co-authored by Frédéric S. Cui supports this engineering approach. Henninger et al. used large-temperature-jump experiments on CAU-10-H and aluminium fumarate to relate progress through adsorption to characteristic times and mass-, area- and volume-specific mean powers. The study shows why ranking materials by an isolated time constant can misrepresent useful power. Savery applies this understanding to shaped contactors, thermal interfaces and complete operating cycles. [T03]

Measured temperature and vapour-pressure responses constrain a dynamic model of loading, heat transfer and flow resistance. Parameter identification is directed at engineering decisions: selecting coating and contact conditions, choosing switching times, and recognising when an aged or contaminated contactor needs a different operating mode. The model retains its tested range and measurement uncertainty. A fitted parameter is useful only when the available measurements distinguish it sufficiently to inform those decisions.

Thermodynamic length provides a local description of dissipation where the measured system supports a linear-response approximation. It helps identify paths that avoid unnecessary gradients for a specified transfer and cycle duration. Larger excursions, hysteresis and switching retain their calibrated nonlinear description. The action applies these methods to product optimisation; it does not depend on creating a universal thermodynamic theory. [T04]

Performance comparisons close the complete-cycle balances and retain comparable initial and final sorbent loading and thermal inventories. This prevents a favourable fragment of operation from shifting regeneration or restoration beyond the evaluation period. Delivered cooling, electrical demand, regeneration heat and external water remain separate measures. The resulting design decisions can then be tested directly on the manufactured equipment.

### Control that preserves service and safe fallback

The existing machine provides the baseline for cyclic operation and thermal management. The product upgrade will develop deterministic supervisory control and model-predictive scheduling, with a bounded reinforcement-learning layer evaluated for setpoints and cycle strategies where it adds useful adaptation. The learning task concerns decisions whose consequences extend across cycles, such as balancing additional adsorption against subsequent regeneration time. Physical limits, safety interlocks, abnormal-condition detection and fallback remain outside the learned policy. Rules and MPC provide qualified alternatives under uncertain state estimates, sensor faults or conditions outside the training domain. The verification plan uses reserved operating histories, equal sensor and actuator access, deliberate disturbances and recorded fallback behaviour. Human operators retain mode selection, alarms, override and safe shutdown. These upgraded predictive and learned functions are development tasks, distinct from the current TRL6 machine's already implemented controls.

### A quantified product specification and transparent comparison

The qualification specification restores the concrete service proposition. It defines a 50–70°C regeneration-heat range and delivered-air targets of 16–20°C with 9–11 g/kg dry-air humidity ratio at applicable rating conditions. Humidity ratio is a water-content measure, not a dew-point temperature. Source temperature, flow, inlet humidity, load and sink condition accompany each rating point. The whole-machine result is calculated at net product-air delivery after working-air diversion and includes the associated auxiliaries.

The electricity and water targets are stated at equivalent useful cooling, with a technically capable reference installation. They will be assessed across the operating envelope rather than inferred from a single favourable component test.

| Measure | Product or commercial objective | Basis for qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Regeneration heat | 50–70°C within the selected operating domain | Measure source, core and return temperatures, flow, duration and available heat; distinguish source supply from material temperature |
| Delivered air | 16–20°C and 9–11 g/kg dry air at qualified rating points | Record net airflow, temperature and humidity ratio together with inlet and sink conditions |
| Cooling electricity | 60–70% reduction as a productisation target | Compare total module and affected plant electricity at the same cooling service, including heat-rejection and treatment auxiliaries |
| External make-up water | 40–60% reduction as a productisation target | Compare incoming external water at equal cooling, after inventory changes, blowdown, treatment and internal reuse |
| Customer payback | 24–36 months for qualifying installations | Use installed cost, operating hours, electricity and heat prices, external water and incremental maintenance |
| Hardware selling-price planning basis | €1,100/kW pilot-series hardware selling-price assumption, progressing towards €900/kW with repeat production | Keep hardware, installation, service costs and cooling-power definitions distinct; validate the scale assumptions through supplier and manufacturing evidence |

The electricity target is consistent with an illustrative equal-service calculation. For a 1 MW cooling duty, a compressor reference at electrical EER 3 draws about 333 kW. A complete module at electrical EER 8–10 draws 125–100 kW, implying a 62.5–70% reduction before any additional installation duty. Qualification will test the complete installed boundary, while reporting the regeneration heat separately. This calculation explains the target; it is not presented as an already measured field reduction.

Likewise, a 40–60% water-reduction target means using 0.6–0.4 m³ of external make-up for a service that requires 1 m³ in the reference system. It does not mean that the module produces 0.4–0.6 m³ of recovered water. The baseline must include the actual cooling configuration, local climate and water-management practice. The commercial analysis uses these measured or modelled service quantities and site tariffs, with lower utilisation and heat availability tested explicitly. The action will replace planning values with accepted product and site results as qualification progresses.

### Competitive advance and timing

Capable alternatives include efficient compressor systems, indirect evaporative equipment, desiccant treatment and heat-recovery systems. Their performance depends on climate, load, water availability and installation constraints. AirTransformer's intended advantage is the combination of low-temperature thermal input, controlled air-side cooling and lower external water demand in a retrofit arrangement that preserves the facility's existing plant.

Low-temperature heat alone is not a unique market distinction. AirJoule's disclosed Prime and Core product families use sorbent cycles, and its Core DH announcement identifies regeneration at 60–70°C. AirJoule also combines water and cooling activities with BitSink. Savery will therefore compare delivered function, air and water interfaces, the complete electrical and thermal boundary, footprint, installation, maintenance and cost. Water-production data from another product cannot be converted into a cooling-energy comparison without a common service definition. [T05]

The timing is driven by the conjunction of an existing TRL6 machine and an adoption problem that needs product evidence. Operators can assess a retrofit against a real cooling duty; manufacturers can qualify drawings and process windows; independent laboratories can test a released configuration. The EIC action connects these steps so that the commercial decision rests on repeatable equipment and accepted service, rather than a further laboratory demonstration of the underlying sorption principle.

### From TRL6 to a qualified and reproducible product

The first gate establishes the current machine's product baseline: configuration, intended use, service boundary, operating domain and the measurement and acceptance plan. Existing records will be organised by configuration so that a later process or controller change can be tested against the correct baseline. The design review sets the remaining material, mechanical, controls and supplier tasks and gives each a responsible engineer and budgeted resource.

The manufacturing gate qualifies the chosen CAU processing and contactor route, assembly sequence and inspection plan. It tests independent preparations and batches against product-relevant acceptance criteria. This gate also establishes which measurements can be performed economically on every unit and which require periodic or sampled verification. Manufacturing release requires reproducibility across accepted variation, not just the best result from one specimen.

The reliability and conformity gate tests the released system under representative thermal, humidity, load and contamination changes. Long-run behaviour, switching transients, abnormal conditions and maintainability are assessed with the complete service boundary. A component change that affects performance, safety or water use triggers the relevant retest. Product claims will identify the configuration, conditions and uncertainty of the result rather than extend a material or component result to every installation.

Final product acceptance brings together the qualified hardware, control and manufacturing versions with the installation and service package. The TRL8 objective is a complete system qualified for its intended use, with production and operational evidence sufficient to support repeat deployment. Customer acceptance will concern useful cooling continuity, agreed air conditions, utility use, alarms and fallback, maintenance access and the responsibilities needed for safe operation. The incumbent system remains available throughout commissioning and relevant trials.

Testing will use ASHRAE 143-2024 for the applicable steady-state indirect evaporative measurements, supplemented by a protocol for the complete cyclic machine. The metrology plan will assess relevant air-side procedures from EN 13141-7, recognising its residential ventilation-unit scope and documenting any adaptations. Calibration will use laboratories with the relevant ISO/IEC 17025 competence and measurement traceability. Method use and traceable calibration will be distinguished from certification of the entire machine. The conformity work will determine the applicable product legislation and tests for the actual installation and electrical equipment; the technical file, risk assessment and declaration follow the released design. [T13]

Climate resilience is an explicit acceptance question. The test matrix will combine hot conditions with constrained electrical and water resources and will record the service that the product retains under those constraints. It also includes low heat availability, unfavourable humidity and warm sinks. Where the product cannot supply the full assigned duty, its permitted operating modes and the retained-plant response form part of the installation design. This makes the resilience claim measurable and gives operators a practical basis for using the technology during the conditions that matter most.

# 2 Market

## 2.1 Market opportunity

### Cooling continuity is a purchasing requirement

AirTransformer serves facilities that must keep cooling through heatwaves while operating within electrical and water limits. Our initial customers are data-centre owners and operators with an identifiable air-side cooling duty and access to suitable low-temperature heat. Their investment decision combines continuity of service, installed cost, annual operating expense and the ability to integrate equipment into a working facility. A cooling system that saves energy but increases the risk of service interruption will not meet this purchasing requirement.

This need is growing alongside digital infrastructure. The IEA projects global data-centre electricity consumption to rise from 485 TWh in 2025 to approximately 950 TWh in 2030. These figures describe whole facilities; AirTransformer addresses the cooling service allocated to its configuration. CBRE reports continuing European capacity growth alongside limited grid availability and construction delays. The European Commission also identifies cooling-water demand and electricity use as material sustainability issues for data centres. Together these trends create demand for equipment that can provide useful cooling with less electrical power and external make-up water. [M1–M3]

We initially address two purchasing situations. In an existing urban facility, the operator may need to add or retrofit a defined cooling duty without increasing its grid connection or external water requirement proportionately. In a new facility or extension, the designer can integrate available heat, heat rejection and air treatment before construction. The first route requires manageable disruption and compatibility with retained equipment. The second allows the interfaces to be designed together. Savery's qualification process covers both, while the existing cooling provision supports continuity during installation and commissioning.

The owner or developer approves investment, facilities engineers define interfaces, procurement allocates contractual responsibility and the operator accepts and maintains the installation. Savery provides the integrated equipment and application engineering needed to connect these decisions. Our offer states the assigned cooling capacity and outlet conditions, heat-source and sink requirements, auxiliary electricity, water balance, maintenance access and acceptance test. Data-centre liquid-cooling architectures can coexist with an assigned air-side duty; we do not present the product as a replacement for every cooling loop in a facility.

Site qualification is a joint engineering and commercial decision. Usable heat must be available at the required temperature, capacity and operating times. Heat rejection, air conditions, space, pressure drop and maintenance access must fit the installation. Water savings are assessed against the actual reference cooling and water-use arrangement. The same site must also offer an economic case strong enough to support procurement. Assessing these conditions together avoids counting a site as serviceable because it meets only one attractive criterion.

### A quantified initial serviceable market

For commercial planning we define an initial annual serviceable market in 2031 covering urban colocation development in Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin. These five markets provide a bounded entry geography across Germany, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, France and Ireland. The planning case excludes North America, industrial process applications, campuses and the retrofit installed base. Those routes provide expansion potential as configurations and delivery capability mature.

CBRE's third-quarter 2025 report projected 624 MW of full-year new capacity across these five markets: Frankfurt 227 MW, London 193 MW, Amsterdam 31 MW, Paris 84 MW and Dublin 89 MW. This is a dated capacity forecast, rather than a list of AirTransformer purchase orders. We use a rounded 600 MW annual capacity flow as a flat planning reference for 2031, without assuming further growth. Our commercial case allocates 10% to an air-side cooling duty and assumes that 25% of that duty jointly meets the heat-source, sink, climate, installation and economic requirements. These are Savery's planning assumptions, to be tested through account qualification. [M4]

The resulting 15 MW of serviceable annual cooling duty is valued at a planning installed price of €1,200/kW, giving an initial annual SAM of **€18 million in 2031**. The price case comprises €1,100/kW of pilot-stage hardware and €100/kW for installation and application engineering. It supports a capacity-based market estimate and customer-economic calculation; an individual offer will reflect its configuration and responsibilities. We do not infer a released physical machine rating from a 100 kW commercial planning equivalent. At joint qualification of 10–40%, the same flat-flow calculation gives €7.2–28.8 million. The range makes the significance of qualification explicit rather than implying that every new data-centre MW is addressable.

We target €0.6 million of sales within this same C05 geography and installed-service scope in 2029, €1.2 million in 2030 and €1.8 million in 2031. These correspond to 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 MW of accepted cooling duty at the planning price. The 2031 target is **10% of the €18 million initial SAM**. It requires product qualification, repeatable manufacture and delivery capacity, followed by customer conversion; it represents planned sales rather than secured orders. Maintenance revenue is assessed separately on contracted installed equipment and is excluded from both sides of this market-share comparison.

The wider opportunity includes suitable retrofits, further European markets and North American deployments, followed by qualified campus and industrial uses. Our company-wide growth model covers a broader product and geographic mix. Its €180.89 million revenue forecast for 2032 is therefore not used as the numerator against this narrowly defined C05 SAM. This separation allows the initial market-entry plan to be assessed on its own engineering, commercial and manufacturing assumptions.

### Competition and the customer's alternative investment

Customers can retain compression cooling, improve economisation or water reuse, procure another desiccant or sorption system, or change their cooling architecture. AirTransformer must demonstrate value against the alternative the customer could actually install. The decision depends on useful cooling delivered at the interface, net electrical and thermal demand, external water withdrawal, space, capital cost and lifetime support.

AirJoule's current Prime disclosure describes water and dry-air production using low-grade waste heat, while its Core DH offering addresses industrial dehumidification with recovered water. The company reports a first full-scale Prime at Newark and a shipment for a European data-centre deployment planned for the fourth quarter of 2026. These disclosures show that low-temperature heat use alone is insufficient differentiation. Prime water-production metrics and Core DH humidity-control metrics also require their own comparison boundaries; they do not establish a matched AirTransformer cooling comparison. [M5]

Transaera addresses MOF-based outdoor-air treatment. Mojave offers liquid-desiccant air handling, including hydronic configurations. Munters provides established air-treatment and data-centre cooling alternatives. Atmospheric-water-harvesting suppliers address a related resource need. Our differentiation rests on the integrated air-side cooling service, compatibility with site heat and heat rejection, material and contactor engineering, whole-installation resource performance and accountable delivery. Productisation will make these differences testable through agreed performance maps and acceptance procedures. [M6–M8]

Savery retains product authority across application engineering, critical process specifications, assembly, control configuration and release. This provides a single technical responsibility for interfaces and performance, while qualified manufacturing and installation partners extend execution capacity. Batch records and field-service findings feed corrective action and controlled updates. For a customer deploying across several sites, repeatable commissioning, spares and maintenance are as important as the efficiency of an individual demonstration.

## 2.2 Business and revenue model and growth strategy

### Equipment and service with a defined acceptance boundary

Our initial revenue model combines equipment, installation, application engineering and paid maintenance. The customer purchases a cooling duty delivered under agreed conditions, with corresponding thermal, electrical and water interfaces. Savery defines the supplied equipment and the customer or third-party infrastructure needed to complete the installation. For configurations that recover liquid water, the offer allocates collection, treatment, storage, metering and use qualification explicitly. ### Customer economics under explicit operating conditions

The purchasing case combines avoided electrical consumption, external make-up water and the cost of the useful regeneration heat, together with maintenance. A calculated example illustrates the economic conditions behind our 24–36-month payback objective. For an assigned 100 kW cooling duty operating 8,000 hours annually, useful cooling is 800 MWh/year. A reference electrical EER of 3 requires 266.7 MWh/year of electricity. A product electrical EER of 8–10 gives 80–100 MWh/year, reducing electricity by 166.7–186.7 MWh/year. These are scenario calculations using an engineering performance range, not a universal measured saving.

At €0.35/kWh of electricity, that reduction is worth approximately €58,300–65,300 annually. The example allows 800 MWh/year of regeneration heat at €0.01/kWh, costing €8,000. A reference external-water requirement of 1.5 litres per kWh of cooling and a 50% reduction give 600 m³/year of avoided withdrawal, worth €2,400 at €4/m³. After a further €2,000/year service allowance, net annual savings are approximately €50,700–57,700. A €120,000 installed investment then gives approximately 25–28 months' simple payback. The result lies within the 24–36-month objective under these stated assumptions.

We will calculate each customer's case using its actual load, operating hours, tariffs and source conditions. Lower electrical tariffs or utilisation, purchased heat with a higher opportunity cost, or additional integration work can extend payback. Retained cooling, heat rejection, water treatment and all auxiliaries enter the comparison. Water already recovered or reused by the reference system is credited consistently. Avoided grid or plant expansion is considered only against an identified alternative investment and is not counted a second time as operating savings.

## 2.3 Broader impact
